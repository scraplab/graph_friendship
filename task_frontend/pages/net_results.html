My Results


Thank you for participating in this study!

What was the purpose of this study?

The purpose of this study is to learn more about the factors that influence the formation of social connections and social networks. Human social networks are known to demonstrate the "Matthew effect" - the (socially) rich get richer, and the poor get poorer. We are interested in the process behind this phenomenon. Are people merely attracted to connecting with popular people - as in preferential attachment - or do other forms of social capital also influence these decisions?

What were our hypotheses?

We hypothesize that several different forms of social capital related to network structure might influence people's decisions about who to form social connections with. In particular, we are testing the effects of four different types of network centrality on people's choices: degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality. These forms of centrality are described in detail below.

How was this study conducted?

On each trial of the task, we showed you a naturalistically structured network generated using a geometric random graph algorithm. The simulated people (newtork nodes) naturally differed in terms of their centrality, so we could measure the extent to which the choice you selected had higher centrality than nodes in the network as a way of assessing how much each form of centrality influenced your choice.

Did we tell you everything?

We never lie to participants on MySocialBrain.org: active deception is not a part of any of our research. However, in some studies we make use of "incomplete disclosure" - meaning that we do not tell you about some aspects of a study in advance. Typically this takes the form of not disclosing our experimental manipulation or research hypotheses. We do this primarily to avoid biasing your responses with demand characteristics. In the case of the current study, we did not describe the various forms of centrality in detail before the study because we did not want to bias you towards using any of them to make your judgements.

Why is this study important?

Social networks have many effects on people's lives, influencing people's health, happiness, and knowledge of the world. To manage these effects, we must first understand the dynamics which give rise to the structure of naturalistic human networks. This study will allow us to learn how a social network's existing structure will influence its future development.

What do my results mean?

We have quantified the extent to which four different forms of network centrality influenced your choices in the experiment. You can read descriptions of each of these types of centrality or view the different types of centrality on an example network below to get a better intuition for them. Note the the numerical results you receive are normalized so that you can compare the effects of different forms of centrality on the same scale.

  • Degree Centrality: simply the number of connections possessed by each node. Analogous to a person's raw popularity in a human social network.
  • Closeness Centrality: distance between nodes is defined by the shortest path between them (in terms of number of discrete links). The closeness centrality of a node is equal to 1/(sum of distances to all other nodes).
  • Betweenness Centrality: is equal to the proportion of all shortest paths between nodes that pass through a particular node. Can be a valuable property in human social networks because people can leverage their connection to different groups a variety of ways.
  • Eigenvector Centrality: is a bit more complicated to compute (see link above), but is based on the intuition that an central node is one that is connected to other central nodes. Closely related to algorithms used by Google and others to sort search results. This measure may not work for very small networks, so try building up a reasonably sized network before clicking this button.
